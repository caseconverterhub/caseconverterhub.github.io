What each case means

UPPERCASE turns every letter into a capital. It is the right choice for acronyms, warning labels, legal headings, and short attention-grabbing labels — but whole paragraphs in uppercase are hard to read and, online, read as shouting. Use it in short bursts.

lowercase removes every capital. It suits casual messages, hashtags, usernames, file names, and code, and it is the fastest way to calm down a paragraph that arrived in all caps: convert it to lowercase first, then apply sentence case to rebuild it properly.

Title Case capitalizes the first letter of the important words in a heading — nouns, verbs, adjectives, and adverbs — while small connecting words such as the, and, of, and in stay lowercase unless they open or close the title. It is the standard for headlines, book titles, and article headings. Our full title case rules guide explains exactly which words qualify.

Sentence case capitalizes only the first word of each sentence, plus proper nouns you restore yourself. It is the default style of everyday prose and, increasingly, of modern web design — most major style guides now prefer it for interface headings because it reads friendly and scans quickly. The differences are subtle but real, and our comparison of sentence case vs title case walks through them side by side.

Capitalize Each Word is title case's simpler cousin: literally every word gets a capital letter, including The, And, and Of. It is common in product names, signage, form labels, and some social media captions where a uniform look matters more than style-guide purity.

aLtErNaTiNg CaSe alternates capitals and small letters letter by letter. It has no place in formal writing, but it is widely used for playful social posts, memes, and mocking-quote formats. Handle with care — it is deliberately hard to read.

camelCase, PascalCase, snake_case, and kebab-case are the naming styles of programming and file organization. If you write code, name variables, or label files, you will meet all four daily; our explainer on what camel case is shows where each one belongs.

Remove Extra Spaces tidies text copied from PDFs, emails, and web pages: double spaces collapse into one, stray spaces before punctuation disappear, and lines are trimmed. Reverse Text flips your text backwards character by character — fun for puzzles, palindromes, and social posts.

When to use title case

Title case earns its keep anywhere a heading has to look deliberate and polished: newspaper and magazine headlines, book and film titles, blog post titles, presentation slide headings, and the titles of reports and white papers. Readers subconsciously treat a title-cased heading as a name — a thing with importance — which is why marketing pages and book covers rely on it.

Reach for sentence case instead when the heading is really a short sentence, when your brand voice is casual, or when the heading contains a product name or technical term whose own capitalization you must preserve. Mixing the two across one website looks careless, so pick one style per site and apply it consistently — our FAQ covers the most common edge cases, and the detailed title case rules settle arguments about words like “with,” “from,” and hyphenated compounds.

Writing style notes: capitalization done well

Capitalization is small typography with outsized effects. A capital letter signals “this matters” or “this is a name,” so every unnecessary capital dilutes the signal. A few habits will keep your writing clean:

  • Capitalize names, not categories. “Mayor Alvarez” takes capitals; “the mayor” does not. The same goes for companies, products, days, months, and languages — but not seasons or school subjects unless they are languages. Our guide to capitalization rules in English lists the full set with examples.
  • Do not capitalize for emphasis. If a word needs weight, rewrite the sentence, use bold in digital text, or shorten the line. Random capitals mid-sentence (“We offer Premium Quality Service”) read as noise.
  • Be consistent inside a document. One heading style, one treatment for job titles, one spelling of a product name. Consistency is what readers actually perceive as professionalism.
  • Watch text pasted from elsewhere. PDFs, spreadsheets, and AI tools each impose their own habits. A quick pass through this converter — lowercase everything, then rebuild with sentence or title case — gives you a clean, uniform starting point.

Title Case Rules: Which Words Get a Capital Letter?

Style guide · 6 min read

Title case is the capitalization style of headlines and titles: capitalize the words that carry meaning, lowercase the small grammatical glue. Almost everyone agrees on the principle; the disagreements live in the details. This guide gives you the rules in the order you will actually apply them, then notes where the major style guides part ways.

The core rules

1. Always capitalize the first and last words. No matter what they are. “Something to Live For” ends on a capitalized preposition because it is the last word, and “The” is capitalized at the start even though it would be lowercase mid-title.

2. Capitalize all nouns, pronouns, verbs, adjectives, and adverbs. This includes short verbs like “Is” and “Be” — a common mistake is lowercasing them as if they were glue words. In “What the River Is Telling Us,” both “Is” and “Telling” are capitalized because both are verbs.

3. Lowercase articles: a, an, the. Unless rule 1 applies, “the” in the middle of a title stays small: “The Lord of the Rings” capitalizes only the first “The.”

4. Lowercase short prepositions. Words such as in, on, at, by, for, of, to, up, off stay lowercase in most styles. The dividing line is length: the Chicago Manual of Style lowercases prepositions of any length, while AP style capitalizes prepositions of four letters or more — so “Through the Night” in AP, “Through” still capitalized as it exceeds three letters, but “With” becomes “with” in Chicago and “With” in AP. When in doubt, the four-letter rule (capitalize prepositions of four or more letters) is the most widely followed compromise.

5. Lowercase coordinating conjunctions: and, but, or, nor, for, so, yet. “Rock and Roll,” “Slow but Steady.” Subordinating conjunctions such as as, if, than, when divide the guides; capitalizing them is the safer modern choice.

Hyphens, colons, and other traps

Hyphenated compounds: capitalize the first element always, and capitalize later elements unless they are articles or short prepositions. “Mother-in-Law,” “Step-by-Step Guide,” “Run-Down Buildings.” A prefix like “re-” or “e-” stays lowercase: “Re-Entering the Market,” “E-Commerce Basics.”

Subtitles after a colon: the first word after a colon is always capitalized, whatever it is: “Deep Work: Rules for Focused Success in a Distracted World.” The same applies after a dash in most headline styles.

Proper nouns keep their own spelling. “iPhone,” “eBay,” and brand names with unusual capitalization stay as the brand writes them, even at the start of a title — although at the very start of a sentence, most editors rewrite to avoid “iPhones are…” openings.

How the big style guides differ

AP (journalism) capitalizes words of four letters or more, including prepositions. Chicago (books) lowercases prepositions of any length and is the most detailed on hyphens. MLA (academic) is close to Chicago but capitalizes subordinating conjunctions. APA uses title case only for titles of works in reference lists, and sentence case for article titles — a frequent source of confusion. None of them is “wrong”; pick the one your publication, school, or company uses and stay with it.

Machines are excellent at the routine 95% of title case; the remaining 5% is editorial judgment, and it is exactly the 5% readers notice.

Sentence Case vs Title Case: Which Should Your Headings Use?

Style guide · 5 min read

Open two websites at random and you will likely see two different heading styles. One says “How to Plan Your First Vegetable Garden.” The other says “How to plan your first vegetable garden.” The first is title case, the second sentence case — and the choice quietly shapes how professional, friendly, or formal your writing feels.

The two styles, precisely

Title case capitalizes the first letter of all major words — nouns, verbs, adjectives, adverbs — and lowercases articles, short prepositions, and conjunctions. Sentence case treats a heading like a sentence: only the first word, proper nouns, and names get capitals. Everything else follows the normal rules of prose.

Same heading, both styles Title Case: A Beginner's Guide to Keeping Bees in the City
Sentence case: A beginner's guide to keeping bees in the city

Readability

Sentence case is marginally easier to scan because the shape of the line matches ordinary sentences, and readers process headings as micro-sentences. Title case, however, makes individual keywords pop — useful in tables of contents and search results where readers hunt for nouns. In practice, the readability difference is small; consistency matters far more than the choice itself.

Tone and tradition

Title case carries the authority of print: newspapers, books, and academic journals have used it for centuries, so it reads as formal and established. Sentence case is the native style of the modern web — Google, Apple, Microsoft, and the BBC's digital services all use it for interface text and most headlines — so it reads as current, plain-spoken, and approachable. A law firm and a language-learning app should probably not make the same choice.

The technical-term problem

Sentence case has one real hazard: headings full of names and terms whose capitalization is fixed. “How to sync your iPhone with iCloud” works, but “Installing Docker on Ubuntu with Nginx” needs three editorial decisions in title case too — proper nouns are capitalized in both styles, so this is a wash. Where sentence case genuinely wins is headings containing acronyms or code, because there are fewer capitals competing for attention.

A simple decision checklist

  • Formal publication, book, report, or press release? Title case.
  • App, website interface, help center, or product UI? Sentence case.
  • Blog or magazine? Either — but check what your three closest competitors do, and match the convention your readers already expect.
  • Team of many authors? Sentence case is easier to apply correctly, because the title case rules have more edge cases to get wrong.

Whichever you choose, apply it everywhere: page titles, headings, buttons, and image captions. Then use the converter to normalize older content — paste a heading, convert, and paste it back. Ten minutes of normalization makes an entire site feel newly edited.

What Is Camel Case? (And Pascal, Snake and Kebab Case)

Explainer · 5 min read

Computers are unforgiving about spaces. A file named “holiday photos 2024” breaks old tools, and a variable named “user name” is simply illegal in most programming languages. Writers of code solved the problem by gluing words together and using capitalization — or underscores, or hyphens — to show where each word begins. The resulting styles have animal names and strong opinions attached.

camelCase

In camel case, the first word is lowercase and every following word starts with a capital: userName, totalPrice, numberOfItems. The humps of the capitals in the middle are said to resemble a camel. Camel case is the default for variables and functions in JavaScript, Java, and many other languages, and it appears in brand names too — iPhone and eBay are camel case that escaped into the wild. Our converter produces it with the camelCase button: paste “number of items” and get “numberOfItems.”

PascalCase

Pascal case is camel case with the first word capitalized too: UserName, TotalPrice, ShoppingCart. Named after the Pascal programming language, it is conventionally reserved for bigger things — class names, components, and types — so a reader can tell at a glance that ShoppingCart is a blueprint and shoppingCart is one specific cart.

snake_case

Snake case keeps everything lowercase and separates words with underscores: user_name, total_price. It is the house style of Python and Ruby, and of database column names everywhere, because it stays perfectly readable without any capitals at all. A shouting variant, SCREAMING_SNAKE_CASE, is reserved for constants — values that never change.

kebab-case

Kebab case uses hyphens instead: user-name, total-price. The words are the meat, the hyphens the skewer. Kebab case owns the web's addresses — URLs like caseconverterhub.github.io/title-case-rules — because hyphens are what search engines recommend as word separators in links, and because hyphens never require the shift key.

Which one should you use?

  • Writing code? Follow your language's convention: camelCase for JavaScript variables, snake_case for Python, PascalCase for classes almost everywhere.
  • Naming files? Kebab-case or snake_case, all lowercase — safest across every operating system and web server.
  • Writing a URL or slug? Kebab-case, always.
  • Naming a product feature? That is branding, not code — but notice how many tech brands borrow camel case to look engineered.

All four styles are in the converter alongside the prose styles, so you can turn any plain phrase into any convention in one click. If capitalization in ordinary writing is your bigger headache, see the capitalization rules guide next.

Capitalization Rules in English: The 10 Rules That Cover Almost Everything

Grammar guide · 7 min read

English capitalization looks arbitrary until you see the pattern: capitals mark beginnings and names. Everything else is detail. These ten rules cover the overwhelming majority of real-world writing; each comes with the mistake people actually make.

The rules

1. Capitalize the first word of a sentence. Including after a full stop inside abbreviations sparingly — and the first word of a quoted sentence: She asked, “Where are the keys?”

2. Capitalize proper nouns — names of specific people, places, and organizations. “River Thames,” “the University of Oxford,” “Nike.” The generic words stay small when they stand alone: “the river,” “a university.”

3. Always capitalize the pronoun “I.” English is unusual here; it is never “i think.” This is the single most common capitalization error in informal writing.

4. Capitalize days, months, and holidays — but not seasons. “Monday,” “October,” “Diwali,” but “spring,” “summer,” “autumn,” “winter.”

5. Capitalize languages and nationalities. “She speaks Urdu and French.” School subjects follow the same logic: “history” is lowercase, but “English” is capitalized because it is a language.

6. Capitalize titles of people when they come before a name. “Dr. Chen,” “President Ahmed,” “Aunt Maria.” When the title stands alone or follows the name, it goes lowercase in most styles: “the president,” “Maria, my aunt.” Family words are capitalized when used as a name — “I told Mother” — but not with a possessive: “I told my mother.”

7. Capitalize regions, not compass directions. “He moved to the South” (a region) but “drive south for two miles” (a direction). Same with “the West” versus “the west wind.”

8. Capitalize the first word and major words of titles of works — books, films, articles, songs. This is title case, and it has its own full rule set in our title case guide.

9. Capitalize historical eras and events. “the Renaissance,” “World War II,” “the Bronze Age” — but generic references stay small: “the war,” “ancient times.”

10. Do not capitalize for emphasis or importance. “Quality,” “Service,” and “Best Prices” do not earn capitals by being things you care about. This “significance capitalization” is the mark of amateur business writing; resist it.

A repair workflow for messy text

Inherited a document where the capitals are chaos? Do not fix it word by word. Paste it into the converter, drop it all to lowercase to reset every decision, then apply sentence case to rebuild the sentence starts. Finally, scan once for names — people, places, brands, languages — and restore their capitals by hand. Three passes, and even the messiest draft reads clean. For headings, finish with the sentence case vs title case decision and apply one style throughout.
